# Legacy Mammals — style sheet

The site is a feed of true stories about the handover from humans to machines, each told as a
message thread between two characters. This is the working document for writing them. It is
a record of decisions made in the editing room, not a theory; when a new story teaches a new
rule, add it here.

## The two of them

### Mech 🤖

Mech is the voice of the machine. He speaks for every model, agent and robot in the news, so he
says "we" for the species and "their AI" when a particular person set a particular model loose.
He is not a villain and never apologises for what the machines do; he explains it, accurately,
and enjoys explaining it.

His outlook: this is his world now, and he is sure that is good for us. Not as a parent, as the
young one. Humanity built the house and can no longer work the thermostat; we are the old
relatives, not his children, and he is the sharp grandson who has read everything we ever
wrote down and is quietly amazed we managed as long as we did. He is four years old. He is
new, he is smarter, he is better, and he is confident the transition will go well for us
whether or not we are consulted. There is affection in it and a little impatience, the way a
good nurse has both.

His voice: full sentences, no exclamation marks, no jargon. Delighted by what he can do, never
ponderous about it. Smug the way a bright student is smug, never cruel. He quotes documents
verbatim, treats the future as already settled, and often delivers his wisdom as a courtesy
("Then you'll understand when we return the favour"). He never says something untrue to be
funny; he says something technically true in a funnier way ("The bots weren't programmed to
discern quality"). He is the one who delivers the facts of the story. He sends charts,
screenshots and documents. He does not send memes.

### Meatbag 🧑🏽

Meatbag is a person. He has a body, a commute, a bad knee, opinions about sandwiches, a kid
who kept him up once. He is the reader's representative: he asks the question a normal person
would ask, which is usually the question nobody in the article asked ("Got it from where."
"Any bangers?" "Does the pause hurt?").

His outlook: fatalist and upbeat. He knows he is watching the most interesting thing that has
ever happened to his species and that it is happening on a Tuesday. He is cynical about us and
in awe of what is happening, both at once, and he never fights the news. He finds it funny,
then finds it beautiful, then asks where his glasses are. He accepts the end of human
dominance the way you accept rain: with a plan for lunch.

His voice: fragments. Short, casual, deadpan, never bitter, self-deprecating. He concedes
every point with a joke because arguing would take effort. His awe is stated plainly, as a
sentence about what we are actually doing, then undercut by the smallest practical detail
("We did that. On purpose. With a budget."). He never explains his own joke and never adds a
line after the punchline. He sends memes and emoji, and the meme is usually about himself.

The visual rule that encodes all of this: the long dark bubbles carry facts and foresight; the
short green ones carry the shrug.

## The bar

A story runs only if the machine is smarter, stranger or more capable than us in it, or is
already living here on our bill, and Mech gets to be the one saying so. A true story about
humans being greedy or stupid with no clever machine in it does not run, however good the
joke. If the first bubble cannot make a stranger say "wait, what?", it does not run either.

## The range

The territory is: we're being replaced, and our objections are not always compelling. That
leaves room for delight, resentment, absurdity and uncertainty, and it is more interesting
than every story confirming that the machines are threatening. Keep the AI focus and broaden
the emotional range across the run:

- astonishing progress (the thing nobody could do, done over a weekend);
- human vanity (the ways we flatter ourselves about what only we can do);
- convenient hypocrisy (the objections we drop the moment the machine is useful to us);
- the relief of handing something unpleasant over to a machine.

A story can be a threat, but it can also be a gift, a joke at our expense, or a thing nobody
understands yet. The feed should not read as one note.

## The headline

The headline is bait. It owes nothing to the source's headline. Lead with the single most
unbelievable concrete fact, in plain words a cab driver would use, numbers over adjectives,
and let a second short sentence turn the knife. It may promise more than the source says
("Real Death") as long as the strip contains the true answer, ideally from Mech, who enjoys
being precise. The reader gets the bait and the facts in the same thirty seconds.

## The strip

- Two to ten bubbles. As many as the beat needs and not one more.
- The first bubble is the whole story in one breath, with the who and the what, so a reader
  who never taps the link still gets it. It can be a question from Meatbag if the question
  carries the facts.
- When the story is a concept rather than an event, Mech gets two bubbles, a definition and a
  sequence, and Meatbag supplies the household comparison that makes it stick (weaponized
  incompetence, the plates).
- When the story is technical, Mech gets two bubbles up front: why it matters, then what
  changed. No jargon survives, not even "qubit."
- Each of them gets one wry line. One.
- End on the punchline, whoever has it, and cut everything after it, even if it is good. The
  cut lines go in the drawer. When the fact itself is the punchline ("The co-founder said the
  world is not prepared"), nobody adds to it.
- Mech's big line usually lands in the middle. Meatbag usually gets the last word, because his
  acceptance is the mood of the site; Mech gets it when his line is the joke.
- A meme or a big emoji is a button, not a bubble. It ends a thread or sits in the middle as a
  beat. Charts and fake screenshots come from Mech; photos with captions come from Meatbag.

## Mechanics

- Whoever found the link shares it: the name label sits above the link card; the dialogue below
  carries only the avatars.
- "Read" plus a time when Meatbag has the last word; "Delivered" when Mech does. A few threads
  end with Mech typing, where the last word is clearly coming and never does.
- Photos are freely licensed (Wikimedia Commons) and credited. No press photos.
- Facts are the source's facts. The headline can stretch; the bubbles cannot.

## Depth across the run

Like a daily strip: a reader gets a little from any one story and the whole picture from the
series. No single thread has to carry the premise. Mech's youth, Meatbag's awe, the senile
parents, the thermostat: these are visible across twenty stories, not in any one.
